Ask Sale Wala

One symbol, built from the three things you actually sell, and the system that carries it onto a signboard, a carry bag, a delivery carton and an invoice. Everything here is vector, prints in a single colour, and holds together whether it is 20 pixels wide or three feet across a shopfront.

01The symbol

Three plates seen edge-on, stacked widest-first, held inside a brass rim. It is deliberately the simplest true thing about the shop: goods stacked and ready to be carried home. Nothing to explain, nothing to decode — which is exactly what lets it work on a fascia board at thirty feet and a marketplace avatar at thirty pixels.

The rim

Crockery

The gold line around good dinnerware. It closes the mark into an emblem — and an emblem reads as a business that intends to still be here in ten years, which is the single most valuable thing a new shop can borrow.

The stack

Handloom

A stack of plates and a stack of folded cloth cut the same silhouette. One drawing covers both halves of the range honestly, without shrinking either into a decorative motif.

The brass plate

The promise

The top of the stack is the piece that gets handed across the counter. Brass marks it, and it is the only warm note anywhere in the system — which is what makes it register.

02Scale & clear space

The symbol is built on a fixed grid: the rim sets the diameter, and the three plates sit at a constant interval with a constant taper. The gaps between plates are open ground, not white shapes, so the mark holds on kraft board, on a photograph and in a single-colour stamp. Keep clear space on all four sides equal to one quarter of the symbol’s diameter.

04Colour

Vat indigo is the dye of Indian handloom; brass is the metal of the Indian kitchen. The pairing belongs to what you sell, and it deliberately avoids the terracotta-and-cream that most of this category is already wearing — on a crowded marketplace listing page, that difference is the whole point.

Indigo carries roughly 70% of any surface, porcelain the rest. Brass never exceeds about 10% — a rim, a rule, one word. The moment brass covers a panel it stops reading as metal and starts reading as a discount sticker.

06The weave

The emblem says what you sell; the weave says how it is made. A plain weave — warp passing under and over weft, the one move that turns thread into cloth — tiles seamlessly across packaging, bag gussets, tissue paper and shelf edging. This is where the handloom side of the business speaks, and it means a customer can recognise your parcel from across the room without reading a word on it.

08Rules

Always

  • Use the supplied files. The symbol is drawn on a fixed grid; redrawing it by eye breaks the taper between plates.
  • Give it room. A quarter of its own diameter as clear space, minimum, on every side.
  • Reverse it on dark. Porcelain plates, bright brass rim and crown — never indigo on a dark ground.
  • Go one-colour when the process demands it — embossing, stamps, single-plate cartons, embroidery.

Never

  • No effects. No gradient, bevel, glow, drop shadow or outline on the symbol.
  • No re-colouring into festival palettes, and no closing the gaps between the plates.
  • No stretching. Scale the lockup proportionally or not at all.
  • Not straight onto a photo. Place it in a solid indigo or porcelain block first.
  • No brass panels. Accent only — it is a rim, not a background.
